## You can indeed use the state of lucid dreaming to enhance the effectiveness of motor learning.

### Daniel Erlacher:

> This research began with my doctoral dissertation. When I defended my doctoral dissertation, I became interested in whether lucid dreaming could be used for motor skill learning. I attempted to address this question at various levels. So, we had to conduct studies in which we sought out lucid dreamers who practiced lucid dreaming and gave them a task to perform and practice in their dreams, and then assess whether dream practice affected waking state performance. Thus, we conducted several studies on this topic and were able to demonstrate that for simple movements, such as finger tapping, you can indeed use lucid dreaming to enhance motor learning efficiency. Additionally, we surveyed athletes to determine if they experienced lucid dreams and, if so, how frequently. They also reported using lucid dreams for motor skill learning in their everyday activities. We also obtained interesting results, showing that at least 20% of the population we surveyed frequently experience lucid dreams. And among them, I believe 20% also used them to achieve sports performance. Furthermore, we conducted interviews with athletes who claimed to frequently use their lucid dreams for training.And in these interviews, we also obtained several good results that show, from a qualitative perspective, that people really use the state of lucid dreaming for motor activity in sports, for different types of anxiety, for training, or anything else. So, basically, these are two levels that may be most interesting to you. And then, we also conducted several studies on a more basic level of the mechanisms of why the practice of lucid dreaming should work and enhance performance.

### ASRP.media

> I am interested in your opinion on the following question. Do you think it is possible in the future to use the practice of lucid dreaming not only for physical training of athletes but also for other types of learning? For example, for learning foreign languages, memorizing new words, and other disciplines.

### Daniel Erlacher:

> Good question. But I always say that I study athletes. I am not a psychologist or an expert in this field. But fundamentally, the same mechanisms should work for studying more semantic and narrative material. Hmm, yes, this should be possible. But of course, before starting to repeat material in lucid dreaming, you should first study something during wakefulness during the day. Because, for example, imagine studying the Chinese language without ever familiarizing yourself with a single word during wakefulness. So what will you repeat and practice in your dream? Thus, you will need full language practice and learning during the day, and then perhaps you will be able to reproduce these processes in your dreams, improving your skills, which also works for motor learning.

Often, it was associated with maintaining natural human biorhythms: when we wake up early in the morning and go to bed at sunset, when a person spends a lot of time in nature to improve sleep quality and more often induce lucid dreams. Doesn't it seem to you that the ability to see lucid dreams may be a natural human ability that has been lost under the influence of industrialization and an unhealthy lifestyle in cities?

### Daniel Erlacher

> You have a piece of text that needs translations. For instance: Oh, this is a good question! But perhaps it should be answered by psychologists knowledgeable in the field of ethics. Hm, there are some scientists and old studies that claim this could be true. For example, the Senoi people and, I don't know, that's just one example that immediately comes to mind. They assert that there are individuals for whom lucid dreaming occurs naturally. That is, the state of lucid dreaming is natural for them, while the dreams we experience are unknown to them. But at least that's what I know. There was one woman... I forgot her name, I read about it a long time ago. She went to that village and conducted detailed interviews with those people. And then it turned out that it was true, that lucid dreams are a normal state for them. So, first of all, let's say we find evidence that some people from their natural environment, who are not subject to the norms of industrial society or something similar, should they have more lucid dreams? It doesn't seem like it. But maybe we just haven't looked thoroughly enough. And another thing is that we, to some extent, lost awareness in dreams during that period. I don't know. It's hard to say. It's really difficult to answer this question.But, at the very least, one could expect that in earlier literary works there would be much more mentions of lucid dreaming. But, it doesn't seem to be the case.

## Can the practice of lucid dreaming unite people from different cultures and different parts of the world?

### ASRP.media

> So, in one of your studies, you compared the results of lucid dreaming among groups of athletes from different parts of the world. If I'm not mistaken, it was an Asian group from Japan and China, as well as groups from Europe. There wasn't a significant difference in the results you obtained. So, do you think that the practice of lucid dreaming could become a practice that brings people from different cultures together and perhaps brings a little more peace to the relationships between people from different parts of the world?

### Daniel Erlacher

> Not sure. I mean, the Japanese study was inspiring. At the time, I had a few colleagues in Japan, so we tried to conduct a joint study and expected that there might be more lucid dreams in Asian cultures because they are mostly traditional and more inwardly oriented towards the state. But it turned out not to be the case, at least according to the results of our survey. But it's hard to say, so maybe the cultural difference is not easily translatable. For example, the same meaning of questions. It could be biased. I mean, we tried our best to make the translations very accurate, and we also cross-referenced with local literature, but it was surprising that they had fewer lucid dreams than their European counterparts, anyway. So, getting back to your questions, I think talking and discussing our dreams is a good common ground for communication, as one of the topics to talk about. Because everyone has dreams. And thus, we can use it, for example, in the morning during breakfast, to discuss our dreams. And it can be, in general, a process accessible to all people.And, at least according to the interest of researchers around the world, it seems that dreams are a topic that is very interesting for the Chinese, Europeans, Americans, and residents of South America. So yes, there is a common ground.

## The future of industries related to lucid dreaming.

### ASRP.media

> This is a significant part of culture for many people around the world, and I believe it could be one of the reasons why we now have a lot of modern gadgets that help achieve lucid dreaming. And year after year, their number keeps growing: there are special applications, special watches, glasses. How do you think this industry can develop in the future? Will it have a future and will it become practical?

### Daniel Erlacher

> Most devices were invented 30-40 years ago when Stephen LaBerge conducted experimental and initial research in this field and essentially came to the question of how to induce lucid dreams. So, one idea is that someone should give you a cue during the dream that you are dreaming. And then you think about how this cue can appear in the dream. So, one idea was visual cues. Another one was auditory cues. But you always face the same problem. That is, you need an intensity that is strong enough to intrude into the dream content, but it should not be too high because then you will wake up. Thus, finding the perfect intensity is very difficult, and that is why none of these stimuli provide a 100% guarantee of lucid dreaming. So, it is very challenging and needs to be tested. Currently, the impact on more sensory organs is being tested: olfactory senses, bodily sensations, and so on. But the same problem arises - finding the right threshold of intensity. And the second problem is that if the cue signal is integrated into the dream, the dreamer must recognize it and draw the appropriate conclusion. And that is also a problem.So, you still need to make some cognitive effort to realize. Masks, practices, or rehearsals really have a strong associative connection between this impulse-cue in a dream and the knowledge that it is a dream. And I think it will take more time before we get the perfect device inducing lucid dreams.

## Personal experience of lucid dreaming and the search for inspiration.

### ASRP.media

> As I understand it, this is also a combination, like in sports. If we want to achieve a result, we can use some tools or equipment to keep our body in good condition. But discipline, mindset, and technique in performing exercises are also important, as well as our focus during training. So, with lucid dreaming, it's a similar situation. I have seen some of your research on lucid dreaming, but there is much more. What makes you repeat research on this topic again and again? What makes it so interesting for you? Perhaps you have personal experience with lucid dreaming that inspires you so much?

### Daniel Erlacher

> Yes. At first, it was like that. I'm not very good at recording dreams, and I'm also not the most skilled lucid dreamer. So I had to try and practice for a long time to see my first lucid dream. And then, of course, my eyes opened, and it was really cool, and I thought, 'Oh, I need to do more of this.' In other words, I had lucid dreams periodically, but as you said, you need to practice and perform reality checks throughout the day. If you do it right, you get more lucid dreams. But if you stop practicing, then you also decrease the frequency of your lucid dreams. There was a time when I had many lucid dreams, and I trained a lot in the dream state. Meanwhile, now I'm more lazy and want to have a device or technique that allows me to have lucid dreams at will. And that still motivates me to explore lucid dream induction. But now the question of exploration is also beyond this basic level. So, we are interested in how the dream environment is formed, especially the dream body. Sensory information coming from the body in the dream state is obviously not input information for the 'dream body'.So, why do we beat around the bush, and how much interference is there between these two phenomena? Can we stimulate not only dreaming but also the sleeping body? And if so, how will it affect dreaming? These are the things that interest us, including communication. Can we communicate with a dreamer while they are aware of their dream? And can they respond to us from within the dream? All these questions are currently at the center of our research.

### ASRP.media

> How do you think, if you manage to find a way to induce stable lucid dreams using devices or their combination with practices, how can we use this in life? And I don't just mean sports. For example, can we use lucid dreams to improve creative skills, such as drawing?

### Daniel Erlacher

> Yes, but here the question arises again. I always say that I am a scientist in the field of sports. But since I have been asked this question many times, we asked Lucid Dreamers what they usually use the state of lucid dreaming for. And the first study was an open-ended study, and it turned out that in 80% of cases they use it for entertainment, and entertainment means several things: to play, explore the dream world, or something similar, but it's mostly for pleasure. Then - dealing with nightmares and getting inspiration for creativity or reverse action, or solving problems in a dream. And then in fourth or fifth place is training skills, such as sports skills or other skills, for example, playing the piano or something similar. Thus, people use lucid dreaming for a variety of things. But, first and foremost, it's always about enjoyment, it's a playground, it's a matrix that they can use to simply do whatever they please.

## VR vs Lucid Dreams.

### ASRP.media

> So, perhaps this could be a good emotional practice against some kind of depression and sad mood? I remember one of your researchers describing how the culture of sleep affects our emotions, that athletes felt happier when they had a culture of practicing lucid dreaming. What do you think, can the practice of lucid dreaming be used for people who, for example, get very tired at work and maybe for some reason do not have many interesting activities in their lives, perhaps because of the place they live or just such a period in their life? In this way, they can use lucid dreams for entertainment and to refresh their consciousness.

### Daniel Erlacher

> Yes, that's a good question. I mean, the problem can still be how to achieve lucid dreaming. Can we give them a reliable technique for inducing lucid dreams? Meanwhile, it seems like we're kind of bypassing that time. It's much easier to buy a good VR system, and then you can do the same things in a lucid dream. That is, without all of this, without sensory input. Yes, that's one of the drawbacks, but essentially it's the same idea, and many people use the metaverse or its analogues to get exactly what you described. They just want to escape the routine of everyday life. But lucid dreams can still be obtained, but, as I said before, you'll have to spend time and effort to achieve that. We still don't have an easy way to access lucid dreaming. In VR, it's much easier.

### ASRP.media

> It seems to me that the only question is a matter of time, because in fact we sleep every night. And it doesn't matter if we have lucid dreams or not, we still spend 6 to 8 hours in bed. And with VR and video games, it always takes up a part of our waking life. So the question of finding balance arises.

### Daniel Erlacher

> But if it takes you one hour of practice to do it. (laughs) Yes, it really is a question of inducing lucid dreams.

### ASRP.media

> This is certainly a debatable question. But I want to say that when we spend some time, one hour, practicing lucid dreaming, it is somehow related to a healthy lifestyle. So, we should go outside, do physical exercises. Usually, doctors recommend this to people - go outside, be physically active, take a break from gadgets, and not stimulate our brain before sleep with phones and similar things. What do you find most interesting in all these studies? You have conducted many studies, so which one do you like the most and which one do you find the most interesting? Not necessarily for scientists, but for yourself.

### Daniel Erlacher

> And yet. There are many things. Yes, I like everything! But most of all, sleep lab research - they are the most complex and impressive because you can already start communicating with the participants. Currently, we are conducting a study in which there are special emitters in the lab, and when we electrically stimulate the sleeper's forearm and move their arm, and then ask the dreamer, 'How does this activation manifest in your dreams?' And it's quite interesting because we have no idea what will happen. Of course, we have some ideas, but there are also those that really resemble research and science as it is. When you don't know what can really happen. So let's just see what happens when we do this, let's ask the Dreamer what happened to them. It's amazing!

> Не уверен. Я имею в виду, что японское исследование было вдохновляющим. В то время у меня было несколько коллег в Японии, поэтому мы попытались провести совместное исследование и ожидали, что в азиатских культурах может быть больше осознанных снов, потому что они в основном традиционные и более внутренне ориентированы на государство.
