After defending his doctoral dissertation in social anthropology at the University of Bristol, Alexis spent a year and a half among the indigenous Ngobe-Bugle people in Panama, studying their culture, customs, and way of life. He paid special attention to the role of dreams in their everyday life and spiritual practices. During his stay among the Ngobe-Bugle, Alexis explored closely related topics such as community formation, politicization, gender relations, crafts, myths, and cosmological narratives. He discovered the profound significance that dreams hold for this community and how they use them to gain knowledge, healing, and interaction with the spiritual world. This experience enriched his understanding of lucid dreaming and became a pivotal moment in his academic career. In our exclusive interview, Alexis shares his impressions of living among the Ngobe, discusses the place of dreams in their culture and spiritual life, and explores his vision of a possible integration of the dream and waking worlds.

He also addresses current issues in social anthropology related to the perception and interpretation of dreams in different cultures and traditions. Reflecting on his own experiences and research, Alexis emphasizes the importance of comparing knowledge obtained from different cultural contexts to enrich our understanding of the mechanisms of dreams and their potential in the fields of science, psychology, and philosophy.

## Social anthropology, world of spirits and dreams.

### ASRP.media

> Dear Alexis, for a long time you, as an anthropologist, scientist, and researcher, have lived among one of the indigenous peoples of Panama. You even defended your doctoral dissertation based on these studies. The name of this indigenous people is Ngobe-Bugle. Could you briefly explain the essence of these studies, the importance of studying this people, and what makes them unique?

### Aleksis Karkotis

> Yes, thank you for the opportunity to talk about the Ngobe-Bugle people and indigenous peoples in general. It was a great honor and opportunity for me to live and work with the Ngobe-Bugle people in Panama as part of my doctoral degree in social anthropology at the University of Bristol. This took place from 2006 to 2012.
> In social anthropology, there is a special methodology that distinguishes it from other social sciences. It is called 'participant observation', which essentially requires you to get off your couch, travel, live with people, and interact with the cultures you are studying. In this way, you participate and observe the culture and its bearers in a dialectical sense, and try, as much as possible, to see the world 'from the natives' point of view', as early anthropologists used to say.
> The word 'native' at the beginning of the century referred to the indigenous population, but today it can refer to any social group in modern society. For example, if we study lucid dreaming practiced by a particular group, anthropology will be needed both for the practice of lucid dreaming and for observing the social aspects of this practice.
> Now, after this prologue, let me tell you about the Ngobe-Bugle, with whom I lived for 18 months, in their own semi-autonomous territory, the Ngobe-Bugle comarca.
> Ngobe is an indigenous group living in western Panama, near the border with Costa Rica. They were one of the first indigenous groups to encounter the Spanish five hundred years ago when they arrived on the continent, and one of the few who survived the subsequent conquest.
> In the last century, when systematic persecutions and wars subsided, they experienced a demographic boom, so today their population is about 350,000 people, of whom 200,000 live in their own semi-autonomous territory called the Ngobe-Bugle comarca.
> Buglé is another group of indigenous people, about 20,000 individuals, who coexist peacefully with the Ngöbe. Ngöbe-Buglé Comarca is a vast territory, approximately 10,000 square kilometers, which is equivalent to the size of Cyprus. It is a tropical region with high biodiversity and majestic tropical mountains, as it is crossed by the Central Cordillera, with numerous rivers and exquisitely beautiful long coastlines. There are no roads, electricity, or modern infrastructure here. The Ngöbe live in scattered communities and hamlets, with 200-300 people in a decentralized, egalitarian, and acephalous society. It is extremely peaceful and tranquil.
> During my 18-month stay in Ngobe, I studied various interconnected topics such as community formation, politicization, gender relations, crafts, myths, and cosmological narratives. As for their cosmology, which we are discussing today, the 'traditional' religious beliefs of the Ngobe about the world are more implied than explicitly expressed.
> Ngobe is what can be called 'animists' in Western understanding, as they perceive the entire physical world as filled with spirits, both good and evil, engaged in a constant spiritual warfare. The spirit world coexists with the physical world, much like the dream world.
> The central aspect of anthropology as a discipline is to avoid fully embracing a "Western-centric approach" when studying the "others" who are not Western.
> For example, in the West, we have a philosophical and religious understanding of the world, the exposition of which is not applicable to other cultures, especially indigenous tribal societies. Western psychology is not as directly applicable to other cultures as Westerners would like.
> For example, a man once told me during a group discussion about spirits and animals - 'Alexis, we don't know why we, the Ngobe, can see spirits and white people can't, but that's how it is. We know that you don't see them, we know. But we don't know why we can see them and you can't'.
> Ngobov's ontological relationships with various domains are blurred, as epistemologically they occupy the same dimension. For example, a butterfly visit may not mean anything, but it can signify that someone, perhaps, is thinking of you and has prompted it to come. A firefly in your house may indicate that someone is planning to visit you from afar. Other animals, especially snakes and eagles, may possess a spirit. Encountering a spirit or an 'animal-spirit' can be interpreted as either caused by the individual through their thoughts and actions or sent by another person, maliciously or not, who is far away. Spirits, entities in dreams, trees, insects, animals, and humans coexist in one dimension and do so as 'personalities' that are clothed in a specific form and interact (through their realities) with each other according to their cosmological perspective in what is a cosmological theater of alliances and wars. This is what Eduardo Viveiros de Castro calls 'multinatural perspectivism' (1998), but it is a lengthy discussion.

## Social Anthropology, World of Spirits and Dreams.

### ASRP.media

> This nation has a completely specific and atypical attitude towards dreams and everything related to the field of dreaming. The "Advanced Scientific Research Projects & Association of Sleep Research Projects" is particularly interested in this topic and direction as a scientific research group in the field of sleep. Please tell us more about its uniqueness and how it differs from the perception of the modern European individual.

### Aleksis Karkotis

> The morning greeting in Ngobe sounds like "Mo boko ninio?", which translates to "What did you dream last night?". A common response is "Niaka", which means "nothing". Not that nothing was dreamt, but nothing of social significance. A shortened version of this morning exchange, which can be heard in the village, is "Niantore", which also roughly means "nothing". Thus, in the morning, Ngobe people say "nothing" to each other. Dreams were dreamt, but not the kind that is considered important to talk about. If a person has a vivid symbolic dream that wakes them up in the middle of the night, they may wake up all the elders of the family, who will gather around the fire and drink cocoa to discuss it. If the interpretation is not reached in the morning, the person will visit the local wise man, whom we call a shaman. The shaman will provide their interpretation and guidance.
> Dreams are very important in Ngobe society. If a Ngobe, for example, has a nightmare, then the next day he or she will not engage in any activities. Most likely, they will stay at home and reflect on what they experienced until they understand it. Until they have beautiful dreams again.
> Anxious thoughts have a highly negative impact on the psyche of the Ngobe. For example, if a Ngobe woman has a bad dream (kyobyo), she most likely will not weave her bag (kiga) the next day. The same goes if she sees a spirit (chela) that scares her. The reason is that in the chakare bag, they carry infants up to three months old, food, firewood, and other household items, and anxious thoughts will weave into the threads of the bag and transmit this negative energy to its contents - her child, food, firewood, clothing. That's why she will never weave if she has a bad dream. Additionally, women believe that in order for the kigi threads to become perfectly white, a woman must be in a uplifted mood and in good spirits. The same goes for a man who repairs or builds his canoe. He will not do it if he is not in an uplifted mood. Or visiting an agricultural field. Negative thoughts attract negative spirits. And this is a pattern observed among indigenous peoples around the world.

### ASRP.media

> What traditions, rituals, techniques, and methods of dreaming exist among them? What is the most unusual thing you have seen while living there in their environment?

### Aleksis Karkotis

> Cocoa. Simple, untouched food, such as tubers and rice. Chants. Dances. Long and peaceful conversations by the fire in the hut at night. You see, in the absence of electricity, the day follows its natural rhythms. In the West, as soon as the sun sets, we turn off the switch and turn on the electric light bulb. They don't have that, and they don't do that. From noon, everyone in the community starts, so to speak, a night journey. It's dark, quiet, and very gentle. They don't use phones, they don't watch TV. Their minds are not so much troubled by cruel news. There is no Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, and so on. If their agricultural field requires care, they can work without a break for two weeks. But if there is no work, the indigenous people do not feel obliged to do anything. Like we, Western people, do.
> In addition, the Ngobe - and this is true for many indigenous peoples - do not follow the 8-hour sleep and 16-hour wakefulness pattern adopted in the West. They divide their day into multiple segments of sleep and wakefulness. For example, they sleep in their hammock at 6 PM for an hour, wake up, and go to sleep again at 10 PM. They wake up at 4 AM, go to sleep again at 7 AM, and so on. They sleep for 6-8 hours a day, but break these hours into segments. Thus, wakefulness and sleep follow each other like waves. And cocoa... They drink a lot of homemade cocoa.
