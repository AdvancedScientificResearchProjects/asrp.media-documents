## Interview with a representative of the "Joint Institute for Nuclear Research" in Dubna

**ASRP.media:**

In 1989, American citizen Bob Lazar shocked the scientific world by claiming the existence of an element with atomic number 115 — more precisely, its stable isotope, which, according to [Lazar](https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/bob-lazar.htm%20) had unique properties and was used in top-secret government projects. At that time, this element was known by the temporary name **"Ununpentium"** (Ununpentium), and in scientific circles it was called **"Unaptanium"** — a hypothetical superheavy metal, capable, according to Lazar's claims, [to generate antigravitational effects](https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/element-115.htm). His statements, made in the interview, became the subject of heated debates: some considered them fantasy, others — a prophecy ahead of its time.

Interestingly, element 115 later "penetrated" even into popular culture: it was mentioned in the legendary computer game *Half-Life 2* (developed by Valve), where it appeared as a key component for the operation of alien technologies. And also — in the game *X* (the developer of which is still a subject of discussion).

But the most surprising thing happened in 2010: at the **Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna** (Russia) an international team of scientists indeed synthesized element 115, officially confirming its existence. It was named **"Moscovium"** (Moscovium, Mc) — in honor of the Moscow region, where Dubna is located.

What of Lazar's claims was true, and what was not? Where is the line between secret developments and scientific discoveries? To answer these questions, our editorial team began a **large-scale journalistic investigation**. And today, after many years of secrecy, we are ready to provide **exclusive materials**.

In the classified archives of the alliance **ASRP** (*Advanced Science Research Projects*) there was an interview recorded on **September 28, 2021**. For many years we did not dare to publish it — it was too significant **politically, defensively, technologically, and scientifically sensitive** a topic. However, after recent hearings in the US Congress on issues of **UAP** and the wave of declassification of government documents, we, **concealing the names of the participants**, finally decided to publish it.

**ASRP.media:**

Please tell us about the synthesis of new elements of the Mendeleev table, those that do not exist in nature, — starting from the 114th and 115th, up to [oganesson](https://ru.wikipedia.org/wiki/%D0%9E%D0%B3%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B5%D1%81%D0%BE%D0%BD). > Yes, of course. By the way, oganesson is named after Academician Yuri Oganessian, under whose leadership these studies were conducted. The elements were discovered at the turn of the 20th–21st centuries, namely: at the very beginning of the 2000s. Attempts to synthesize them were made since the 1970s in different countries, but it was in Dubna in the 1990s that this path was completed. From the late 1990s, a series of experiments began, resulting in the discovery of the five heaviest elements — from the 114th to the 118th.
>
> Moreover, they were discovered in very small quantities — literally single atoms: one per week or even per month, like oganesson, for example. But this was enough to prove that they can be obtained and their basic properties studied.
>
> This can be compared to the first human flight into space. Gagarin's flight lasted only 108 minutes, but it opened up an entire era. Undoubtedly — this is a key event for humanity, just as here — with the discovery of these elements, new opportunities have emerged.

And what tasks are currently facing researchers?

JINR Dubna:

> The most fundamental, of course, is to study their chemical and nuclear properties, shape, mass of nuclei, and much more. This is a huge area of work! There is another important mission: to continue the Mendeleev table. There is an opinion that it is not finished, and it is believed that at least a few more elements can still be discovered, for example, 119 and 120. These tasks require radically new technology, which is tens of times more advanced than what has been used in our country and in the world so far. It exists and is called "*Factory of Superheavy Elements". This is a completely new complex: from the foundation to all the equipment. The goal is to extract the maximum from what modern engineering thought allows. The first experiment was conducted on it in November 2020 — the synthesis of the 155th element, Moscovium. And it was a real breakthrough, because in 40 days, with interruptions, about 70 atoms were already obtained!*

**ASRP.media:**

And what is the lifespan of such atoms?

JINR Dubna:

> The longest-lived of the known isotopes exist for about a second, even a little less. This is already enough to study their properties. But the most important thing is their quantity. One, two, or three atoms cannot be studied, but when we are talking about tens, hundreds, and thousands, we can talk about a qualitatively new level of experiments.

**ASRP.media:**

Have there already been experiments with other elements?

JINR Dubna:

> Yes, of course, the second experiment — the synthesis of the 114th element, Flerovium. About 100 atoms were obtained there. This confirms that the technology is gradually reaching design parameters. As Rutherford said: if a new installation worked immediately, then a mistake was made somewhere. We are going step by step, bringing the system to perfection.

**ASRP.media:**

And is it already known what the 119th and 120th elements will be called?

JINR Dubna:

> Of course not, it's too early. First and foremost, we need to achieve reliable confirmation of the very fact of their existence. Then, the procedure is quite strict: first, the publication of an article, then an application to the international commission on chemistry and physics. The commission verifies the validity of the results, preferably with a repeat of the experiment in another laboratory. Only after this is the discovering team granted the right to propose a name.

**ASRP.media:**

And all these discoveries were made in Dubna?

JINR Dubna:

> Yes. Elements from the 114th to the 118th were discovered right here, at the "Joint Institute for Nuclear Research."

**ASRP.media:**

And can we talk about the practical application of these elements? For example, is it possible to use their isotopes in fast neutron nuclear reactors?

JINR Dubna:

> No, that's impossible. It's important here to distinguish between fundamental and applied science. Fundamental science seeks to understand, *how the world works*. All practical applications are a byproduct of knowledge. Electricity, the internet, tomography—all of these appeared thanks to research that initially had no utilitarian goal.
>
> So here too: there will be no practical application for the superheavy elements themselves. We obtain single atoms that live for fractions of a second. Even if stable isotopes existed, their quantity would be negligibly small.

**ASRP.media:**

So they don't exist in nature either?

JINR Dubna:

> Of course, they existed, but they disappeared. Elements heavier than uranium do not live long, so they are no longer present under Earth's conditions. But somewhere in the Universe, in catastrophic astrophysical processes — in neutron star mergers, in supernovae — they are still being born. We are just too far away to observe them. For scientists, this is unfortunate, but for ordinary people, I think it's fortunate.

**ASRP.media:**

So, it's like you're reproducing what happens in stars?

JINR Dubna:

> Exactly. We do not create what does not exist in nature, but we replicate natural processes by other means. This is an attempt to answer fundamental questions: what elements are possible at all, where does the periodic table end, does the periodic law hold for superheavy elements.

**ASRP.media:**

And what do you say about the popular version — for example, Bob Lazar's theory that the 115th element could be fuel for spacecraft?
